Introduction

Consider an agent that selects regions of interest (ROIs) from whole-slide images. It works. But every slide starts from zero. When a clinician corrects a mistake, the agent does not keep that lesson. So this essay asks one question: how can a clinical AI agent get better over time?

What is RSI?

Duan and colleagues describe recursive self-improvement (RSI) as AI systems that “turn experience and feedback into persistent changes that improve both their capabilities and the process of future improvement” [1].

The paper defines it through one loop. Experience feeds an improver, which proposes a change to a target. A verifier accepts or rejects it. The accepted change is kept in the state, and the next round starts from there [1]. So an improvement counts only if it is accepted, kept, and reused. It becomes recursive when the change also improves the improver or the verifier.

For every system, it helps to ask three questions. Where does the loop close? What is kept? What stays with humans?

The levels

The paper describes levels of improvement [1]. At each level, people hand one more decision to the AI.

Levels of recursive self-improvement [1]
Level What the AI does In healthcare today
B0 Does the task; nothing is kept Widespread
L1 Applies a fixed update process designed by humans Demonstrated
L2 Helps decide which changes to make Strongest frontier
L3 Chooses what to learn or practise next, aimed at its own weak points L3-like only
L4 Keeps learning from real use after release, and decides which lessons to keep Simulated only
L5 Improves the improver: revises the part that proposes or judges the changes Largely unexplored

At every level, a person still sets the goal and the limits.

What do “L3-like only” and “simulated only” mean?

  • L3-like only. A few systems already use their uncertainty or past experience to choose what to look at next. For example, which evidence to gather, which case to simulate, or which sample to send to an expert. TissueLab [7], for example, uses expert corrections to guide active learning. But it is not full L3, because “their patient populations, task distributions, and admission criteria remain largely externally specified” [1]. Humans still decide what the system can learn from.
  • Simulated only. EvoPatient [8] adapts patients and doctors together, but both are simulated. The paper notes that “the simulator's clinical realism and evaluation criteria remain externally determined” [1]. Real L4 would learn from real patient outcomes over time, and that “remain[s] largely unexplored” [1].

Where medical agents stand

So, where is healthcare today? The paper's answer: L1 “has been demonstrated through case memories, diagnostic rules, and reusable clinical knowledge”. L2 “represents the strongest current frontier” [1]. L3 shows only L3-like elements, L4 exists only in simulation, and L5 is largely unexplored.

Here are the medical agents from the survey that do keep improving across cases [1].

Medical agents that keep improving across cases [1]
Agent Task Level What keeps updating
DxEvolve [3] Diagnosis by dialogue L1 Memory of reasoning patterns
EvoClinician [4] Diagnosis by dialogue L2 Prompt and memory
MACRO [5] Medical imaging L2 New composite tools
Evo-MedAgent [6] Chest X-ray L2 Memory: cases, rules, tool trust
TissueLab [7] Pathology, radiology, spatial omics L3-like Workflows; refines small classifiers
EvoPatient [8] Simulated patients and doctors L4-style Dialogue libraries, question pool

Most are at L2. They update memory, prompts, tools and workflows. Only TissueLab also updates weights, by refining small classifiers from expert corrections. In the words of the paper, “clinical experience can improve future agent behavior without necessarily changing foundation-model weights” [1].

Healthcare is not software engineering

In software, you can try a fix and roll back in seconds. In healthcare, feedback is “delayed, heterogeneous, and confounded”, and “restoring a software checkpoint cannot undo physical or clinical consequences” [1]. In short, healthcare is safety-critical, so safety comes first.

Human decisions or automated AI

Human decisions are the reference, and an expert checks every change. But expert time is limited, and the process can be slow. Automation is fast and can scale up, but it has no ground truth of its own.

Toro-Tobon and colleagues warn that “clinician in the loop” can end up “shifting responsibility for AI safety from developers to doctors” [2]. But they also show where oversight works: in “bounded loops” with clear ground truth, “where human oversight is feasible and meaningful” [2]. So the question is not human or AI. It is where expert time is worth the most.

A realistic next step: what to update

We do not need full autonomy. The next step is to move such an agent from B0 to L1, and then toward L2, while keeping clinicians in control. The VLM stays frozen. What changes is everything around it.

What changes in the ROI agent at each level
Part of the agent Now (B0) At L1: a fixed update rule At L2: an LLM Evolver helps choose
Feedback Clinician rates ROIs, then it is lost Ratings are stored as feedback Same
Diagnosis of misses A person reads the misses Clinician reviews a miss report The Evolver diagnoses the misses
Prompts and guards Edited by hand Edited by hand The Evolver proposes edits
Reference bank Curated by clinicians Updated from rated ROIs Same, with the Evolver choosing entries
Small classifiers Trained once Retrained on expert-confirmed ROIs The Evolver decides when and what to retrain
Keep or roll back Decided by hand Kept only if ratings improve Same
New cohort Not done Clinician signs off Clinician signs off

Harness code and tools stay with humans at every level.

So, how do we evaluate it? We measure two things: the share of ROIs accepted by clinicians, and the expert minutes per slide. We compare the starting agent with the agent after several rounds. The VLM, tools and budget stay the same, and we test on a locked split. If ROIs get better but experts spend more time, the loop has not solved the real problem.

What is still open

The paper names three big challenges and eight research directions [1]. These are the ones that matter most for healthcare.

Open challenges for self-improving medical agents [1]
Challenge The problem, in the paper's words What is needed
Safe updates “retaining an artifact does not establish that later agents benefit from it” Link each update to its evidence and effect; validate before release
Knowing what helped “an observed failure rarely identifies the component that should change” Testable hypotheses and targeted ablations
Fair evaluation Changing the evaluator can “reward exploitation” Keep independent acceptance criteria
Cost “autonomy should be assessed together with the amount and type of human effort required” Report time, cost and review effort

The paper also sets the bar for real RSI: “repeated, attributable, and transferable improvements in the capacity to improve” [1]. In healthcare, the paper notes that “autonomous evolution of the clinical improver is not yet established” [1]. In short, the VLM stays frozen, but the agent can learn from clinical experience, one checked step at a time.
