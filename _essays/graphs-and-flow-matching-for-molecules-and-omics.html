Introduction

This essay asks one question: why do so many models for molecules and omics look the same? They build a graph, run a GNN on it, and use flow matching to move between distributions. MintFlow [10], a model for spatial transcriptomics, has all three. So, why do these choices fit the data? And where do VAE and latent diffusion stand?

Why is the data a graph?

Because the data is made of parts and relations, not a fixed grid.

Nodes and edges for different kinds of data
Data Node Edge
Molecule Atom Chemical bond
Single-cell RNA Cell Similar expression (kNN)
Spatial omics Cell or spot Physical neighbor (kNN or radius)
Gene knowledge Gene Pathway, interaction, ontology term

For molecules, chemistry gives the graph. For cells, we build it ourselves. A common choice is a kNN graph. MintFlow uses k = 5 [10].

This is not new. Wolf and colleagues write that SCANPY “introduces a class for representing a graph of neighborhood relations among data points” [1]. They add that “Typically, SCANPY's tools reuse a once-computed, single graph representation of data” [1].

But kNN is not the only way. RNA-FM predicts bulk RNA-seq from whole-slide images [11]. It groups genes into Gene Ontology pathways and links pathways that share genes. In the authors' words, “we define a pathway graph with an adjacency matrix” [11]. Here the edges come from biology, not from distance.

In short, the graph is how the field already describes cells and molecules.

Why a GNN on top of the graph?

Because a GNN computes what the graph says: each node looks at its neighbors. Gilmer and colleagues call this message passing. Their models “learn a message passing algorithm and aggregation procedure to compute a function of their entire input graph” [2].

This fits for three reasons:

  1. No fixed order. Atoms and cells have no natural order, and a GNN does not depend on one.
  2. Variable size. The same weights work for a small molecule and for a dataset with millions of cells [10].
  3. Local first. Many chemical and cell-to-cell effects act locally. One layer is one step of who affects whom.

MintFlow's encoder is such a GNN over its kNN graph [10].

The limits are known. Li and colleagues show that graph convolution brings “potential concerns of over-smoothing with many convolutional layers” [12]. And a fixed k is a choice, not biology.

In short, once we accept the graph, the GNN is the natural encoder.

Why flow matching?

Because many biology questions are transport questions. Control cells become perturbed cells. One cell state becomes the next. We have samples from both sides, and we want the map between them.

Flow matching learns this map as a velocity field. We then solve an ODE to move samples along it. Lipman and colleagues call it “a simulation-free approach for training CNFs based on regressing vector fields of fixed conditional probability paths” [3]. In practice, the loss is a squared error to the straight line from start to end, x1 minus x0 [4].

Three things make it attractive in biology:

  1. The start can be data. Tong and colleagues state that “CFM does not require the source distribution to be Gaussian” [4]. So the start can be control cells.
  2. No paired cells needed. We rarely measure the same cell twice. OT-CFM pairs samples by optimal transport within each mini-batch. The authors list “inferring single cell dynamics” as an application [4].
  3. Fast sampling. Lipman and colleagues report that optimal transport paths “are more efficient than diffusion paths, provide faster training and sampling” [3].

Examples are growing. CellFlow models single-cell phenotypes under complex perturbations [5]. RNA-FM “formulates transcriptomic prediction as a continuous-time conditional transport problem” [11]. MintFlow runs flow matching in latent space, because flow models on high-dimensional data are “impractical” [10].

It is not a free win. Lyu and Luo note that flow matching methods struggle with “the extreme sparsity of single-cell data” [6].

In short, flow matching fits when the question is how one population of cells becomes another.

So why not a VAE or latent diffusion?

We do not have to choose. Several recent models combine them [9]. The real question is which part does which job.

The VAE is still widely used. Lopez and colleagues used scVI for tasks “including batch correction, visualization, clustering, and differential expression” [7]. MintFlow is also VAE-style, with priors and an ELBO [10].

Latent diffusion is used in genomics too. scDiffusion combines a latent diffusion model with a foundation model [8]. In scLDM, Palla and colleagues train the latent model “using linear interpolants and the flow matching loss” [9]. They also cite CFGen, which “combines scVI with a flow matching model in the latent space” [9].

So the line between latent diffusion and flow matching is thin. Both learn a path in latent space.

VAE, latent diffusion and flow matching compared
VAE Latent diffusion Flow matching
What it learns Encoder and decoder with a simple prior Denoising of latents, from noise Velocity field between two distributions
Start of the path Gaussian prior Gaussian noise Any distribution, e.g. control cells
Sampling One decoder pass Many denoising steps ODE solve, often few steps
Strong at Representation, batch correction, DE High-quality generation Mapping one population to another
Weak at A simple Gaussian prior limits generation [9] Slow sampling, needs a good autoencoder Sparse counts, still young in biology
Single-cell example scVI [7] scDiffusion [8], scLDM [9] CellFlow [5], OT-CFM [4]

The table is a summary of the sources above, not a benchmark.

So, why flow matching for perturbation? A perturbation has two real distributions, before and after. Diffusion starts from Gaussian noise. Flow matching does not have to [4], so it can connect the two directly.

In short, the VAE gives the latent space, and flow matching or diffusion moves things inside it.

Takeaways

Graphs fit because molecules and tissues are parts and relations. GNNs compute on those relations. Flow matching fits because many questions ask how one population becomes another. VAE and latent diffusion are not replaced. They often give the latent space where the flow runs.

Two limits stay open. A kNN graph with a fixed k is a modeling choice. And a map from control to perturbed cells is a prediction. Without interventional data, it is a hypothesis, not a causal result.
